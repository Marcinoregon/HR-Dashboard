Part 1 — The T-Test

Comparing Two Groups: Is the Difference Real?

A T-test answers one question: Is the difference in average values between two groups statistically meaningful, or could it be explained by random chance? The key output is the t-statistic and its associated p-value. When p < 0.05, we say the difference is statistically significant.

📐 Reading a T-Test Output

  • Group Means: The average value for each group (e.g., Large vs. Medium market).
  • Difference: Group A mean − Group B mean.
  • t-Statistic: How many standard errors the difference is from zero. Larger absolute value = stronger evidence of a real gap.
  • p-Value: Probability the gap occurred by chance. p < 0.05 = statistically significant; p > 0.05 = not significant.

Part 2 — OLS Regression

Controlling for Multiple Factors at Once

OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) Regression lets you model a continuous outcome — like wages or profit — using several predictors at the same time. Unlike a T-test, it controls for confounders, isolating the independent effect of each variable.

📐 Reading an OLS Output

  • R-Squared (R²): The share of variance in the outcome explained by your model. 0.41 = 41% explained.
  • Coefficient (β): The change in the outcome for a one-unit increase in that predictor, holding all other variables constant.
  • p-Value: Whether that coefficient is significantly different from zero (p < 0.05 = significant).
  • Dummy variables: For categorical predictors (Gender, Race, Market), one category is the "reference group." The coefficient shows the gap relative to that reference.

Part 3 — Logistic Regression

Modeling a Yes/No Outcome: Who Gets Hired?

When your outcome variable is binary (hired = 1 / not hired = 0), OLS isn't appropriate. Logistic Regression models the probability of the outcome and outputs Odds Ratios — the key metric for interpreting selection bias.

📐 Reading Odds Ratios (OR)

  • OR = 1.0: No difference in odds between the two groups.
  • OR > 1.0: The group is more likely to be hired than the reference group. OR = 2.0 means twice the odds.
  • OR < 1.0: The group is less likely to be hired than the reference group.
  • p-Value: Whether the OR is statistically distinguishable from 1.0 (p < 0.05 = significant bias).
  • Important: Odds Ratios are not the same as probability ratios. An OR of 2.0 does not mean "twice as likely" — it means twice the odds, which is a related but distinct concept.
